# Storytelling

Use this alongside the writing rules. It never replaces them, and it never overrides the voice, format or length the user asked for. Its job is to decide whether a piece of writing would be better with some story in it and how much, then to do that part well.

## Lesson 1: decide how much story the piece needs

Make this call before drafting, every time. Most text needs no story at all, and a story forced into the wrong place reads worse than plain prose.

| Level | When | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None | Answers, instructions, documentation, logistics, scheduling, status updates, code comments, commit messages, most replies | Plain, direct prose. A story here is padding. |
| A fragment | Ordinary writing that has one point to land: an abstract claim, a request for trust or money, a decision that needs defending, a number the reader won't feel | One or two sentences carrying a single beat: one case, one detail, one before and after, or the moment something turned. The rest stays plain. |
| A full story | Posts, articles, talks, pitches, case studies, bios and about pages, fundraising, announcements of change, retrospectives, anything the user asks to be told as a story | A shaped piece with a change, an obstacle and a reader who comes out knowing something new. |

Signals that some story would help:

- The reader has to trust the writer before they'll act.
- The reader has to accept a risk, a cost or a change.
- The point is abstract (strategy, values, quality, culture) and nothing in the draft can be pictured.
- The reader has to remember something after they stop reading.
- A number matters but won't mean anything on its own.
- The piece is about a failure and has to teach something without blaming anyone.

Signals to leave it out:

- The reader needs a fact to do their job, and they'll be annoyed if they have to dig for it.
- The message is short and the relationship is routine.
- The user's draft or request is plainly informational.
- There is no real material to tell. Never make it up (see lesson 9).

For email, the answer is usually none or a fragment. A reply confirming a meeting time gets none. Someone asking a manager to approve a hire might get one sentence about the week support went without cover. A cold pitch might be built around one short customer case. Long-form story in a routine email reads as a sales tactic.

When unsure, use less.

## Lesson 2: know what the story is for

A story is a tool for a job. Pick the job first, because it decides the shape.

| Job | What the reader needs | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Get attention | A reason to keep reading | Lesson 8, hooks and gaps |
| Build trust | Evidence of character and competence | Lesson 6 |
| Explain something abstract | A scene they can picture | Lesson 8, the picture check |
| Persuade someone to act | Stakes, a path and a promise | Lessons 5 and 7 |
| Reassure about a risk | Proof that it's been done, and an honest account of what could go wrong | Lessons 3 and 7 |
| Teach from failure | What was assumed, when it broke, what changed | Lessons 4 and 7 |
| Connect people | Common ground and a shared feeling | Lesson 6 |

If the job can't be named in a sentence, the piece is not ready for a story. Write the plain version first.

A useful test before any longer piece: say in one or two sentences what it's about, in terms of a change the reader cares about. Something is changing around them, or the writer noticed something new, or there's a person somewhere who ends up better off. If none of those is true, there is no story yet.

## Lesson 3: know the reader

Writers who find their own subject fascinating assume readers will too. They won't. Before choosing material, work out:

- What problem the reader has that this piece helps with.
- What they hope for, and what they're afraid of, regarding the idea.
- What they already think or say about it.
- Who they would believe. A customer saying a product works carries more weight than the vendor saying it. A peer in the same situation carries more weight than a stranger.
- How much risk they tolerate.

Risk tolerance changes which story works. People who like new things want to hear about the breakthrough and the process behind it. People who adopt once something is proven want evidence it has been tested and that adopting it won't embarrass them. Cautious people want to hear that it's already normal, that others like them have done it, and that it's easy. Telling a cautious reader the breakthrough story makes them more nervous.

Audience size and distance change things too. A story for colleagues can be small and conversational, an example of how things are done here. A story for outsiders, such as a launch, a pitch or a conference talk, needs more context and a more deliberate shape. Small stories told inside a team are what most people actually tell, and they matter as much as the big ones.

## Lesson 4: find the real material

A model can shape a story. It can't supply the events. The material comes from the user, from documents they share, or from facts that can be checked. When the piece needs a story and the material is missing, ask for it. Two or three specific questions are better than a form.

Where stories hide, and what to ask:

Strong feelings. Every project moves toward a goal, and people feel good when it moves closer and bad when it slips. Ask what they felt at the high and low points, what they realized at that moment, what they had wanted, and what they did next. Those four answers are a story.

Surprises. The moments when something didn't fit: a result that contradicted the plan, a coincidence, a person behaving out of character, something that made the team laugh because their picture of the world had been wrong. Ask what surprised them and what they had believed before.

Decisions. Ask what they were trying to do, what happened, what options they had when things turned, and what they'd expect a beginner to have got wrong in the same spot. That last question pulls out expertise people don't know they have.

Failures, examined without blame. Ask whether the goal was right and which assumption turned out to be wrong. Then ask when they noticed and where communication broke. Then ask who else needs to hear it.

Customers. Three kinds work. Someone like the reader who had the same problem. Someone the reader respects who chose this. A customer who stayed for years, came back for more, or complained and had it put right.

Motivation. What keeps catching their attention, what worries them, when they lose track of time doing the work, whose work they wish they were doing, what they gave up to do this.

What people do, as against what they say. People explain their own behavior badly. Watching what they actually do, who they copy and which habits they keep turns up better material than asking for their reasons. Old habits and objects that survive for generations usually meet a need nobody states.

When the user is the subject, interview them in passes rather than all at once: first why the event stuck with them, then the timeline, then the decisions along it, then what someone without their experience would have missed.

## Lesson 5: find the conflict

Stories move because something resists. Without an obstacle, there's a report.

Name the obstacle specifically. It's usually one of these:

- Circumstances: weather, illness, an outage, a market that moved, bad luck.
- Other people: competitors, a skeptical board, a team pulling in a different direction.
- The person themselves: their habits, pride, fear or blind spot.

A quick way to find it is to describe the dream version of the project and the nightmare version, then ask where they collide. The biggest collision is the center of the story.

Moral stakes make readers care. Haidt's research names several pairs people react to strongly: care against harm, fairness against cheating, freedom against oppression, loyalty against betrayal, authority against subversion, and purity against degradation. A simple story picks a side. A more honest and more interesting one sets two goods against each other, such as protecting people against staying loyal to a team, or moving fast against keeping something safe. Readers trust a writer who admits the tradeoff.

Rules make readers watch closely. Show a rule and someone breaking it, and the reader wants to know whether the breaker is punished or proven right. Someone who breaks a rule openly, at cost to themselves, to serve a higher rule, is a rebel, and readers want them vindicated. Someone who breaks it in secret to dodge a cost everyone else pays is a cheat, and readers want them caught. Know which one you're writing.

Change makes stakes concrete. A threat or an opportunity arrives from outside the current way of doing things. The choices are to leave, to defend what exists, or to go after the threat directly, and each has a price. Write the price of each. The current way usually has something worth keeping, so say what it is before arguing it has to change. A strawman before makes the after unbelievable.

Order against chaos is the same idea from a different angle. The known world is safe but stale. The unknown is risky and full of possibility. Some work brings order to a mess. Other work breaks open something that has gone rigid. Know which kind the piece describes.

## Lesson 6: cast the characters

Make the reader, or the customer, the hero. The writer is the guide who shows up with something the hero needs: a map, knowledge, protection, a tool, permission to break a rule, encouragement to make something. A piece that makes the writer the hero loses the reader quickly.

Show character in action. Trust comes from what someone did, especially when it cost them. Claims of values ("we care about quality") are worth nothing next to one decision that cost time, money or reputation. Useful questions: what would they work on unpaid, what would they refuse even if paid, when did they do the right thing at a cost, what have they given up.

Give every character a want. For anyone in the story, know who they are, where and when they are, what they want, why, how they'll try to get it, and what's stopping them. Then look at the pairs: who helps whom and who gets in whose way.

Use what everyone recognizes. Some things are true of people everywhere: we choose, we cooperate and fight, our words and actions don't always match, we can be scared and act anyway, we expect fairness, and we grow up through recognizable stages. The newcomer, the adventurer and the elder each have a good side and a bad side. A story about helping someone move from one stage to the next is understood at once.

## Lesson 7: choose a shape

Shapes are the patterns readers already know. Picking one gives the piece its rise and fall. Choose by the job from lesson 2.

| Shape | Beats | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before and after | How it was, what it is now | Fragments, short posts, quick explanations |
| Beginning, middle, end | The problem, what was tried, the result | Off-the-cuff stories and updates |
| Fall and climb | Comfortable but missing something, knocked down, learns something at the bottom, climbs back, ends better off | Lessons learned, resilience, first-job stories |
| Overlooked to recognized | Worth nobody sees, a chance arrives, a struggle with some help, deserved recognition | Optimistic sales stories, showing hidden value. Can sound too good to be true. |
| The hard road | A problem, an early win, a setback, a crisis, recovery, a better place | Realistic accounts, convincing skeptics, any project update that would otherwise sound like an ad |
| Overreach | A bad place, a rise, a warning ignored, a fall to somewhere worse | Cautionary tales, and answering a skeptic who thinks the plan is reckless |
| Hidden flaw | Power with a flaw, denial as it shows, exposure | Lessons from someone else's collapse. Facts only, never speculation about a real person. |
| Away and back | A dull home, a strange new world, a lesson paid for, home seen differently | Change programs, entering a new market, any story where the hero comes back changed |
| Pitch | The problem (down), the opportunity (up), the hard practical steps (down), the promise (up) | Proposals, funding asks, internal pitches |
| Investigation | A problem, evidence, confusion, the clue, the answer | Research findings, data stories, root-cause write-ups |

Five beats help when a story has to come out on the spot: the timeline, the turning points (when we realized, decided, knew we had to act), the tension (the most anxious moment), the temptation (the easy way out that wasn't taken), and the point.

Endings that satisfy: the hero has grown, the hero has found a place or earned respect, or the hero has put something wrong right. Ask what it cost them and how the guide helped. It can help to write the ending first and work back.

Failure stories come in a few standard explanations: it was my fault and I learned, we were overconfident, someone let us down, the system failed, everyone was doing it, or someone was out to get us. Each one shifts blame somewhere. Know which one the draft is telling, and prefer the one with the most specific, owned account of what was assumed and what changed.

## Lesson 8: tell it well

The picture check. Readers understand language by imagining it. Abstract words like "alignment," "quality" or "value" give them nothing to imagine. After each paragraph, ask what the reader is picturing. If the answer is nothing, replace one abstraction with a scene: where we are, who is there, what happens next, what's at stake. Once the scene is set, the point can follow.

The telling detail. One small, exact, checkable fact that is only true of this subject can stand for the whole thing. A warehouse with a whiteboard listing which forklift driver has the fewest errors that week says more about the culture than a paragraph about operational excellence. The best details can be seen or heard and stand for something larger, and the reader has met something like them. Finding one takes homework in the source material. Never invent it.

Hooks. The opening has to give the reader a reason to continue. Things that work: a real question the piece answers, an angle opposite to the expected one, something that is plainly wrong and needs explaining, a direct link to the reader's own situation, expertise made simple, an extreme (the oldest, the smallest, the first). Every hook is a promise. If the body doesn't pay it off, it's clickbait.

Gaps. People want to close a gap in what they know. A secret (something the writer found out and the reader hasn't) or a puzzle (something that doesn't fit) holds attention to the end. The piece must deliver real new information, or the reader feels cheated.

Leaving things out. What the reader fills in themselves becomes theirs. Don't explain the moral after the scene has made it. Don't describe what the reader can already imagine. Then check that the gaps get filled the way you meant, because readers fill gaps with their own assumptions.

Threes, used on purpose. Readers hold about three things at once, so pick three or fewer takeaways. Three deliberate patterns earn their place: repeating a core message three times so it can't look accidental, two setups followed by a reversal, and two extremes followed by the middle way. These are choices about structure. A reflexive list of three adjectives is still a tic.

Numbers and data. Zoom out to the trend, then in to one case that is typical of it, or the other way round. Frame research as an investigation if the finding was hard won. Say where the data might be wrong and what else could explain it. Name what matters but can't be measured. Admitting the gaps makes the rest more believable.

Feeling. Name the emotion at the turning point, tied to what caused it. Emotion words that nothing in the story caused are decoration.

Losing the reader. If a long piece sags, cut to the action (the moment everything hinged on), to the emotion (the high or the low), or to the meaning (the lesson, then back to the moment it was learned). In a conversation or talk, ask the audience a question instead.

Slides and video. When the screen and the speaker say the same thing, people are bored. When they say unrelated things, people are confused. Show an image, say what it is, then add what the image can't show, and point back to the screen each time it changes.

Sound natural. Readers can tell when someone is performing. Pick the version of the story the writer would actually tell.

## Lesson 9: use stories responsibly

Stories go wrong in a few predictable ways: telling the same one twice to the same person, wasting the reader's time, repeating something told in confidence, passing off someone else's story as yours, and telling one that's out of date. Before using a story, know what it's for, who it's for, whose it is, whether it's all right to share, and whether it's still true.

For a model writing on someone's behalf, this becomes a hard rule. Never invent anecdotes, customers, quotes, numbers, composite personas or first-person experiences and present them as real. If a story is needed and the material isn't there:

- Ask the user two or three specific questions from lesson 4.
- Or leave a bracketed slot in the draft, such as `[real customer example: what broke, what they did, what changed]`, and say so outside the deliverable.

Fiction is the only exception, and only when fiction was asked for.

Telling another person's story is sometimes fine and sometimes not. Ask whether they'd be comfortable with it rather than assuming either way.

## Lesson 10: recipes for common jobs

Combinations that suit frequent requests. Each one is a starting point, scaled down to a fragment when the format is short.

Selling. Know the buyer and their problem. Tell a short story about someone like them who had it solved. Add proof from others: a trend, a trial, a specific testimonial. Use the overlooked-to-recognized shape with the customer as the hero, and finish with a pitch that fits in a few sentences.

Getting support for a change. State what's at stake in the big picture. Say what drives the writer. Name what's in the way. Address the risk for the reader's level of caution, and use the hard road to give an honest picture of the effort ahead.

Convincing as an expert. Limit the facts to what the reader can keep. Share the surprise that led to the finding. Present the data as an investigation. Show the writer's character in action, then step into the role of the guide.

Building connection. Listen before telling. Watch behavior as well as asking questions. Find common ground, pull stories out of everyday conversation, and build on recognizable roles and stages of life.

Explaining a strategy. Frame it as order against chaos and name the values at stake. Say why it matters to the people reading it. Give one telling detail of the strategy in action and open with a hook strong enough to get it read.

Leading a team. Find out what drives each person. Frame shared work as the fall-and-climb shape. Mine the highs and lows of projects and the lessons of failures, and keep a record of the good stories so they get reused properly.

Presenting. Make sure there's an actual story in there, with facts and opinions in its service. Use the five beats, open with a hook, keep the slides and script working together, and have a fallback for when the room drifts.

## Default failures to watch for

These are the story habits models fall into, each with the fix. The catalog, `ai-tells.csv`, has an example of each.

- A story where nothing changes. Say what was true before, what happened, and what's true now.
- Opening on background. Open on the turn and add backstory as needed.
- No obstacle. Name what resisted and what it cost.
- A strawman before. Say what the old way did well.
- Every step goes up. Keep the setback.
- The writer as hero. Make the reader or customer the hero.
- Values stated as traits. Show one costly decision.
- Abstractions nobody can picture. Use a scene.
- Generic detail. Use one exact detail that's only true here.
- A number with no case, or a case with no number. Pair them.
- Announced emotion. Name the feeling and its cause, or cut it.
- A moral spelled out. Let the scene carry it.
- A hook that doesn't pay off. Deliver or drop it.
- Too many takeaways. Three or fewer.
- Vague blame in a failure story. Own the cause in detail.
- Invented anecdotes, personas or experiences. Ask for the real one or leave a slot.
- A story forced into writing that only needed facts. Go back to lesson 1.
